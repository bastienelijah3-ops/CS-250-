# CS-250- SNHU Travel Project: Sprint Review and Retrospective
## 📋 Portfolio Reflection
### 1. Interpreting and Implementing User Needs
Interpreting user needs requires moving beyond raw feedback to identify high value requirements that expand a client base.In the SNHU Travel project, our Product Owner acted as the bridge between the business vision and the developers by taking raw feedback from focus groups where customers requested "budget limits" or "top destinations" and turning them into a prioritized Product Backlog.

**User stories** are essential to this process because they define the "Why" behind every task using plain language. For example, User Story #4 (Budget Based Search Filter) was successful because it was framed from the end-user's perspective: *"As an end user, I want to set a price limit... so that I can find travel packages that fit my budget"*.This clarity allowed the team to find the best technical solution during the sprint and allowed the tester to create functional test cases that checked for specific behaviors.

### 2. Approach to Development & Agile Processes
My approach to development docused on flexibility and outcome based iterations rather than the rigid, documentation heavy processes found in Waterfall models. In the SNHU Travel project, the Scrum-Agile approach allowed us to pivot when challenges arose, such as a tight five-week deadline.

In my future development work, I hope to incorporate the following Agile processes:
* **Backlog Refinement:** This helps reduce ambiguity in user stories, allowing the Developer and Tester to work much faster.
* **Daily Scrums:** These are essential for transparency, allowing a team to sync work and identify impediments like "Epics" that are too large for one sprint in real time
* .
* **Information Radiators:** Using tools like a digital Scrum Board (Jira) to display the status of tasks publicly creates trust and ensures there are no surprises during the Sprint Review.
* **Sprint Reviews:** These allow the client to inspect the system and provide frequent feedback so the team doesn't waste time building unnecessary features.


### 3. Being a Good Team Member in Software Development
Being a good team member means moving out of silos and embracing constant collaboration. It requires active communication that is specific and reduces wait time for others, such as a developer proactively asking the Product Owner for UI clarifications.

A good team member also understands how their role supports the whole, for instance, our tester didn't just wait for code to be finished but reached out to the Product Owner early to ask for clarification on features, preventing the developer from wasting time on incorrect code. Ultimately, a strong team member fosters a cohesive culture where everyone is aligned on the same goals.
